One Lorry, 1,800 Miles

When you see a photo of a packed lorry, you're looking at the last five minutes of a story that started weeks earlier. Here's what really goes into getting one truck of aid from South London to Ukraine.

By the Bridge2Ukraine team 6 min read

We've now sent 72 lorries to Ukraine. Each one gets a photo, a thumbs-up and a quick post saying it's on the road. What doesn't make it into the post is everything that happened before: the phone calls, the spreadsheets, the Saturday mornings and the late nights. It's not glamorous, but it's the reason the aid gets there at all.

So we thought we'd show you, step by step, what goes into one journey.

It starts with a wish list, not a warehouse

Every cargo begins with our partners in Ukraine. Small, trusted charities in Kyiv, Odesa and Sumy tell us what they actually need right now. One month it's incontinence pads and baby formula for an evacuation centre. The next it's rehab equipment for a hospital that's just taken in 40 injured soldiers.

That list shapes everything. We'd love to say yes to every donation, but space on a lorry is precious, and the last thing a frontline hospital needs is a pallet of things it can't use. Sometimes the kindest thing we can do is say "not this time."

"A lorry is about 13 metres of hope. We try not to waste a single centimetre." Sacha, founder (placeholder quote)

The six weeks before the engine starts

Here's roughly how a single cargo comes together. The timings are a typical example and change a lot from trip to trip.

  1. Finding the goods (weeks 1–3)We contact hotels mid-refurbishment, schools clearing classrooms, gyms replacing kit and NHS suppliers with surplus stock. Then we arrange collections, sometimes one van, sometimes a whole team.
  2. Sorting and checking (weeks 2–5)Everything goes to our hub in South England. We check that it works, check expiry dates and throw out anything that isn't good enough. If we wouldn't give it to our own family, it doesn't go.
  3. Packing and labelling (weeks 4–5)Every box is labelled in English and Ukrainian, and every item is listed. Boxes go onto pallets and get weighed and shrink-wrapped. Heavy things go low and over the axles so the load doesn't shift.
  4. Paperwork (all the way through)This is the bit nobody sees, and it gets its own section below.
  5. Loading day (week 6)Pallets go on, the doors close, the photo gets taken, and the driver heads for Dover.

The paperwork nobody talks about

A lorry full of humanitarian aid still has to cross several borders, and every border wants paperwork. For each cargo we prepare a full packing list (every line, every box), customs declarations, humanitarian cargo documents for Ukraine and a CMR transport note.

It's slow, careful work, usually done at a kitchen table after a hard days job. And it matters: one missing signature can leave a lorry waiting at the Polish–Ukrainian border for days.

Did you know?

  • A typical cargo holds around 26 pallets and 600+ individual boxes.
  • The packing list for one lorry can run to 30 pages.
  • The road journey itself usually takes 3–5 days.

Where the £4,000 goes

People often ask why we fundraise for transport rather than for "stuff". The honest answer is that UK businesses are incredibly generous with goods. What's harder to find is the money to move them.

That £4,000 covers diesel, the Channel crossing, road tolls across France, Germany and Poland, customs agents' fees and paying a professional driver. Not a penny goes on the goods themselves, because those are all donated.

And then it arrives

When the lorry reaches Ukraine, our partner charities take over. They unload, sort again and drive the aid the last stretch to hospitals, schools, bomb shelters and families, often in places a big lorry can't safely go.

A few days later the photos start coming back: a nurse with a box of supplies, a child in a new coat, a thank-you video from a bomb shelter. That's what keeps us going while the next lorry is being packed.
